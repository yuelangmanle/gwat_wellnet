# Figure captions (submission copy)

**Fig. 1.** Long-term monitoring wells and minimum reliable network sizes across aquifer strata. (**a**) Locations of the 29,717 eligible monitoring wells (1950–2026) across the conterminous United States, colored by well trend direction (red, falling water levels, *n* = 18,513; blue, rising, *n* = 11,134; gray, stable, *n* = 70). (**b**) Minimum wells required for ≤ 10% sign-reversal risk over 1975–2025: simulated threshold (colored circle), closed-form prediction from Equation 2 (black diamond), and full network size *N* (open circle). Rows are colored by required retention fraction (green, ≤ 5%; amber, 5–50%; red, > 50%). Closed-form predictions agree with simulated thresholds within a factor of two for 17 of 19 uncensored networks; three weak-signal systems do not reach ≤ 10% risk on the standard grid (predicted retention of 88–94%).

**Fig. 2.** Probability of a regional trend-sign reversal versus retained network size *n* over 1975–2025. (**a**) Uniform random thinning across the six largest principal aquifer strata (unassigned wells omitted; shaded bands show < 10%, 10–30%, and > 30% reversal risk; circles on the 10% line mark *n*ₘᵢₙ). Strong-signal aquifers remain reliable with few wells (Coastal Lowlands, *n*ₘᵢₙ = 11), whereas the Basin and Range aquifer reverses sign frequently at all tested sizes. (**b**) National network (CONUS): uniform random removal (black curve with 95% binomial band) versus spatially clustered removal of 0.5° blocks (orange curve), with the sampling law of Equation 1 (dashed curve). The national trend tolerates random removal down to 297 of 29,717 wells (1%), whereas 7,726 wells (30%) retained in spatial clusters carry the reversal risk of ~180–280 random wells.

**Fig. 3.** Collapse of network-thinning trials onto the finite-population sampling law and the network design chart. (**a**) Observed sign-reversal probabilities across all 216 thinning trials (marker area proportional to *N*; colors as in Fig. 1b) plotted against normalized sample size *c*·SNR·√(*nN*/(*N* − *n*)). The solid curve is Φ(−*x*) with *c* = 1.27 (95% CI: 1.04–1.55) and no fitted shape parameter, where SNR = |β_net| / *S*. (**b**) Network design chart showing contours of required retention (*n*ₘᵢₙ / *N*) for ≤ 10% sign-reversal risk from Equation 2 as a function of SNR and *N*. Points show individual aquifer networks; red shading marks the regime requiring ≥ 90% of existing wells.

**Figure S1** (Supporting Information). Comparison of deterministic and random well-retention strategies in (**a**) a strong-signal aquifer (High Plains aquifer, *N* = 8,160) and (**b**) a heterogeneous aquifer with offsetting local trends (Central Valley aquifer system, *N* = 183). Curves show the absolute slope bias |β̂ − β_net| (m yr⁻¹) versus retained wells *n* on log–log axes under 50 random deletion orders (black curve and gray 95% band), longest-records-first retention (solid red curve), and spatial-spread-first retention (dashed orange curve). In (**a**), none of the strategies reverses the regional trend sign at any tested size, but at 10% retention (816 wells) longest-records-first retention produces 5.4 times the slope bias of random retention (0.017 vs. 0.003 m yr⁻¹). In (**b**), random and longest-records-first orders reverse the reference trend sign below ~150 wells, whereas spatial-spread-first retention preserves the trend sign down to 14 wells (8% retention) at a slope bias of up to 3.1 times the reference slope (see Section 3.5 of the main text).

Summary: > Virtual thinning of 29,717 long-term U.S. monitoring wells shows that regional groundwater-level trend reliability follows a finite-population sampling law, yielding a closed-form minimum network size n_min = Nz²/(z² + (c·SNR)²N) from two archive statistics (SNR and N) and demonstrating that well reductions must be spatially distributed rather than clustered.
